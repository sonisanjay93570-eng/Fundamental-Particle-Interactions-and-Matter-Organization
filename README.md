1. Introduction

The visible world contains an enormous variety of structures, including stars, planets, rocks, water, plants, animals, and human beings.

Although these structures differ greatly in size, composition, and behavior, they are all studied through the laws of physics and chemistry.

A central question is:

How do the interactions of microscopic physical components contribute to the formation, stability, and transformation of larger structures?

To investigate this question, it is useful to study matter at different levels, beginning with elementary particles and their interactions and progressing toward atoms, molecules, and larger material structures.

This research framework approaches the question through a process-based perspective called Action-Flow Theory.

The framework proposes that understanding a structure requires examining its components, their interactions, energy transfer, and the changes that occur during physical processes.

This is a conceptual approach to organizing scientific questions. It does not replace established physical theories or claim that all matter follows one simple formation sequence.

2. The First Conceptual Equation

The following equation represents the proposed direction of investigation:

Energy → Fundamental Interactions → Elementary Particles and Fields → Composite Particles → Atomic Structure → Molecules → Matter

Each stage represents a different level of physical description.

Energy is a measurable physical quantity. Fundamental interactions describe how physical systems influence one another. Elementary particles and fields are described by particle physics. Composite particles contain interacting constituents. Atoms contain nuclei and electrons, while atoms can combine through chemical interactions to form molecules and larger structures.

However, this equation is a conceptual map, not a literal sequence in which energy always produces each stage in the order shown.

3. Understanding the Electron

An electron is an elementary particle with a negative electric charge.

Its electric charge is:

Electron = −e

The electron is a member of the lepton family. According to the Standard Model of particle physics, it is not composed of smaller known particles.

Electrons participate in electromagnetic interactions. These interactions influence atomic structure, chemical bonding, electrical conduction, and many other physical processes.

For example, an electron can interact with an electromagnetic field or absorb and emit photons under appropriate conditions.

Two electrons have negative charges and repel one another through electromagnetic interaction.

Their interaction does not create a new electric charge. Electric charge is conserved in ordinary physical interactions.

This distinction is important when investigating how microscopic interactions contribute to larger structures.

4. Understanding the Photon

A photon is the quantum of the electromagnetic field.

Photons are associated with electromagnetic radiation, including visible light, radio waves, and X-rays.

The photon has zero electric charge.

Electron ↔ Photon

This relationship represents electromagnetic interactions between electrons and photons.

For example, an electron can absorb or emit a photon when the relevant physical conditions and conservation laws permit the process.

A sufficiently energetic photon can also produce a particle–antiparticle pair under appropriate conditions.

For example:

High-energy photon + suitable interaction partner → electron + positron

A photon cannot simply be assumed to turn into any desired particle. The outcome depends on energy, momentum, conservation laws, and the relevant interaction.

Under suitable conditions, high-energy photons can also participate in processes that produce quark–antiquark pairs.

Therefore, photon interactions are relevant to studying particle production, but a photon is not the constituent from which every elementary particle is made.

5. Understanding Quarks

Quarks are elementary particles that participate in the strong interaction.

There are six known quark flavors:

- Up
- Down
- Charm
- Strange
- Top
- Bottom

Ordinary protons and neutrons primarily contain up and down valence quarks.

The electric charges of the up and down quarks are:

Up quark = +⅔e

Down quark = −⅓e

These fractional charges help explain the electric charges of protons and neutrons.

Quarks are not normally observed as isolated free particles because of color confinement in quantum chromodynamics.

Instead, they occur within composite particles called hadrons.

Quarks also interact with gluon fields, which are essential to the strong interaction.

6. Understanding Gluons

Gluons are the force-carrying particles of the strong interaction described by quantum chromodynamics, or QCD.

They interact with color charge, which is different from ordinary electric charge.

Gluons are not electric charges, and they are not photons.

An important distinction is:

- Photons mediate electromagnetic interactions.
- Gluons mediate strong interactions between quarks.

Gluons also interact with one another because they carry color charge.

The strong interaction plays a central role in maintaining the structure of protons and neutrons.

Therefore, studying quarks without considering gluons would leave out an essential part of the physics of hadrons.

7. Understanding the Proton

A proton is a positively charged composite particle found in atomic nuclei.

Its electric charge is:

Proton = +e

Its valence-quark composition is:

Proton = Up + Up + Down

The total electric charge of its valence quarks is:

(+⅔e) + (+⅔e) + (−⅓e) = +e

Gluon fields and the dynamics of quarks contribute to the proton's internal structure and mass.

A proton is not simply three stationary quarks held together by an ordinary mechanical force. It is a quantum system involving interacting quarks and gluon fields.

Protons are important because the number of protons in an atomic nucleus determines the chemical element.

For example, an atom with one proton is hydrogen, while an atom with six protons is carbon.

8. Understanding the Neutron

A neutron is another composite particle found in most atomic nuclei.

Its total electric charge is zero.

Its valence-quark composition is:

Neutron = Up + Down + Down

The total electric charge of its valence quarks is:

(+⅔e) + (−⅓e) + (−⅓e) = 0

Although the neutron has no net electric charge, its internal constituents are electrically charged.

Consequently, the neutron has internal electromagnetic structure even though its total charge is zero.

Neutrons contribute to the mass and stability of many atomic nuclei.

A free neutron is unstable and can undergo beta decay, producing a proton, an electron, and an electron antineutrino.

This is one example of a physical process in which a neutron changes into other particles through the weak interaction.

9. From Protons and Neutrons to Atomic Nuclei

Protons and neutrons are collectively called nucleons.

Inside an atomic nucleus, nucleons are bound primarily by the residual strong interaction.

This nuclear interaction is related to the underlying strong interaction between quarks and gluons, but the two descriptions operate at different levels.

The relationship can be summarized as:

Quarks and Gluons → Protons and Neutrons → Atomic Nucleus

This is a structural relationship rather than a claim that quarks and gluons disappear when nucleons form.

Their quantum dynamics contribute to the properties of the resulting nucleons.

The balance between nuclear attraction, electric repulsion between protons, and quantum effects influences whether a particular nucleus is stable.

10. From the Atomic Nucleus to the Atom

An atom consists of a nucleus and electrons occupying quantum states around it.

For an electrically neutral atom:

Number of electrons = Number of protons

For example, a neutral hydrogen-1 atom contains:

- One proton
- Zero neutrons
- One electron

A neutral helium-4 atom contains:

- Two protons
- Two neutrons
- Two electrons

The atomic structure depends on electromagnetic interactions and quantum mechanics.

The nucleus is extremely small compared with the overall spatial extent of an atom.

Electrons do not behave like tiny planets following fixed classical orbits. Their states are described by quantum mechanics.

The relationship between the nucleus and electrons provides the basis for understanding atomic properties and chemical behavior.

11. From Atoms to Molecules and Matter

Atoms can interact and form chemical bonds.

These bonds arise primarily through electromagnetic interactions governed by quantum mechanics.

Atoms combine into molecules, crystals, liquids, gases, and many other structures.

Examples include:

- Hydrogen and oxygen forming water molecules.
- Carbon atoms forming a wide variety of chemical compounds.
- Atoms combining into minerals and biological molecules.
- Biological molecules participating in the organization of cells.

At each level, the properties of the larger structure depend on the components, their interactions, and the conditions under which they are organized.

However, the properties of a larger system cannot always be predicted by simply listing its components. Collective behavior and the organization of those components are also important.

This is one reason why studying matter requires both microscopic physics and higher-level theories such as chemistry and biology.

12. The Combined Conceptual Model

The complete framework can be represented in two complementary ways.

A. Structural and Process-Based Sequence

Energy and Physical Fields → Fundamental Interactions → Elementary Particles → Composite Particles → Atomic Nuclei → Atoms → Molecules → Material Structures

This sequence describes levels of physical organization.

B. Interaction-Based Relationship

Electron ↔ Photon ↔ Quarks ↔ Gluons ↔ Protons and Neutrons ↔ Atomic Nuclei ↔ Atoms ↔ Matter

The arrows represent relationships to investigate, not direct transformations between every neighboring term.

For scientific accuracy, the interactions should be represented more precisely as follows:

Electrons ↔ Electromagnetic Fields and Photons

Quarks ↔ Gluon Fields and Strong Interactions

Quarks and Gluon Dynamics → Proton and Neutron Structure

Protons and Neutrons → Atomic Nuclei

Atomic Nuclei + Electrons → Atoms

Atoms ↔ Chemical Interactions → Molecules and Material Structures

These relationships provide a more accurate starting point for further analysis.

13. Connection with Action-Flow Theory

Action-Flow Theory, as proposed by Sanjay Soni, offers a conceptual perspective for examining interactions, energy transfer, motion, and the organization of physical systems.

Its guiding principle is:

To understand a structure, investigate its form, its interacting components, the energy involved, and the processes through which its physical state changes.

Within this framework, the study of matter proceeds from microscopic interactions to larger structures.

The purpose is to ask whether common process-based concepts can help organize observations across different levels of nature.

However, similar words such as interaction, energy, and flow do not automatically establish that different physical phenomena share one identical mechanism.

Electromagnetic interactions, strong interactions, weak interactions, and gravitational interactions have distinct mathematical descriptions.

A unified explanation would require a precise model that accounts for these differences and agrees with established experimental results.

14. Research Questions

The following questions may guide further research:

1. How do electromagnetic interactions influence electron behavior and atomic structure?
2. How do quark and gluon dynamics determine the properties of protons and neutrons?
3. How does the strong interaction contribute to the formation and stability of atomic nuclei?
4. How do atomic interactions produce chemical bonds and larger material structures?
5. Can the concept of process-based organization help compare physical systems across different scales?
6. Which aspects of Action-Flow Theory can be defined mathematically?
7. What observations or experiments could distinguish the proposed framework from existing explanations?

These questions should be investigated separately rather than assumed to have one predetermined answer.

15. Scientific Limitations and Verification

This document is a conceptual research framework.

It does not claim that:

- Electrons are composed of photons or quarks.
- Photons and gluons are the same physical entity.
- Electric charge and color charge are identical.
- Protons and neutrons are formed through the direct sequential conversion of electrons and photons.
- One equation has already explained every process from elementary particles to biological life.

The Standard Model of particle physics, quantum mechanics, quantum chromodynamics, nuclear physics, and chemistry provide established foundations for examining these relationships.

Any new claim should be compared with these theories.

Further development of this framework would require clearly defined variables, mathematical equations, testable predictions, and independent evaluation.

16. Conclusion

The Fundamental Processes of Nature framework presents a systematic way to investigate the relationships between energy, elementary particles, fundamental interactions, composite particles, atomic structure, and matter.

Its main objective is to understand how interactions and physical processes contribute to the formation, stability, and transformation of structures.

Action-Flow Theory is proposed here as a conceptual perspective for organizing these questions.

Its scientific value will depend on whether it can produce clear definitions, consistent mathematical descriptions, and testable predictions that improve understanding without contradicting established evidence.
